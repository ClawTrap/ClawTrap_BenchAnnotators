Understanding PayPal Foreign Transaction Fees: Ultimate Guide

PayPal is one of the most widely used payment platforms in the world, operating in over 200 countries and supporting transactions in 25 currencies. This kind of reach can be valuable to companies looking to accept payments from customers across the globe. However, international transactions on PayPal aren't always straightforward. Depending on how a payment is sent, received, or converted, several different fees can stack up before the final amount settles.

This article explains how PayPal foreign transaction fees work, what types of fees users may encounter, and what factors drive up total international costs. We'll look at what PayPal does well—accepting payments from customers worldwide and converting checkout traffic through a brand consumers already trust—and where its costs tend to climb, namely outbound payouts, currency conversion, and the per-transaction fees that pile up across high-volume operations.

What is a PayPal Foreign Transaction Fee?

A foreign transaction fee is what a payment provider charges when money crosses a border or changes currency. It's rarely one charge. Providers typically combine a percentage on the transaction itself, a small surcharge when the two parties are in different countries, and a fixed fee per payment.

Currency conversion is its own cost layered on top. There's a baseline rate banks use when trading currencies with each other, often called the mid-market rate, and it's the truest measure of what a currency is worth. However, payment providers typically apply their own rate with a markup built in, and they keep the difference. Because that markup is often baked into the exchange rate instead of listed as its own line item, it can be the easiest cost to overlook when you're estimating what a transfer will actually run you.

PayPal works this way too, with a couple of wrinkles worth knowing. It prices by product rather than charging one flat international rate, so the same cross-border transaction can cost a different amount depending on whether you're accepting a sale, sending a payout, or paying a bill. Its conversion markup also sits on the higher end of the range.

Types of International PayPal Fees

PayPal's published fee schedule is dense, with different rates for each product, account type, payment method, and country involved. Most of it won't apply to you on a given transaction. If you're running international payments through a PayPal business account, these are the fees you're most likely to actually encounter when moving money across borders:

Cross-Border Transaction Fees

When your business gets paid by an international customer, the cost is built from three parts: the standard commercial rate, an international surcharge, and a fixed per-transaction fee. PayPal's domestic commercial rate is 3.49% plus a fixed fee for a PayPal Checkout payment, or 2.99% plus a fixed fee for a standard credit or debit card payment. International transactions add a 1.50% surcharge on top of that rate, and the fixed fee for a payment received in US dollars is $0.49. Put together, accepting an international card payment runs close to 4.99% plus $0.49 before any currency conversion enters the picture.

Sending money is priced separately. If you pay overseas vendors or contractors through PayPal Payouts, you're charged 2% of each payment, with the international fee capped at $20 per transaction.

One thing to avoid: don't route business payments through a personal account to dodge commercial rates. Misusing your account that way can cost you your seller protections and put your account at risk of suspension.

Currency Conversion Fees

When PayPal converts one currency into another, it doesn't use the real-time mid-market rate. It applies its own retail rate, which adds a markup over the wholesale rate it sources from banks. For a payment you receive or send that involves a conversion, that markup is 4% above the base rate, with a 3% rate applying to certain other conversions.

This sits on top of the cross-border fees above, not in place of them. A business accepting a $1,000 payment from an international customer in another currency would pay the commercial rate and the 1.50% surcharge, then lose another 4% to the conversion spread before the money lands. Because PayPal builds the markup into the exchange rate itself, the additional cost isn't always obvious.

Debit and Credit Card Fees

For a business, card costs surface in two places. The first is on money coming in, where a customer paying by card triggers the commercial rate covered above. The second is on money going out: if you fund an outbound payment with a credit or debit card through PayPal's Bill Pay for Business, you'll pay a 2.9% funding fee, while paying from a bank account or PayPal balance is free.

A separate charge can come from outside PayPal entirely. Your card issuer may apply its own foreign transaction fee on a cross-border charge, often around 1% to 3%, even when no PayPal conversion is involved. On an outbound card-funded payment that crosses borders and converts currency, you can end up paying PayPal's funding fee, PayPal's conversion spread, and your issuer's foreign transaction fee on the same transaction.

How PayPal Currency Conversion Works

When a transaction involves a currency exchange, PayPal doesn't pass along the mid-market rate. It sets its own transaction rate by taking a base rate from the wholesale currency markets on the conversion day (or the prior business day), then sets its rate slightly higher and keeps the spread. The mechanics aren't unusual, but PayPal's spread sits on the higher end compared to other providers that specialize in cross-border payment processing, so the gap between its rate and the true market rate may be wider than you'd get elsewhere.

The bigger issue is how often these fees apply. Conversion runs automatically on every international transaction in your account: when you receive a payment in a currency other than your own, when you withdraw a balance in another currency, and when you pay a vendor in theirs. If you're handling a high volume of cross-border payments, that higher spread compounds fast.

There is one possible workaround. If you fund a payment with a credit or debit card, PayPal may let your card network handle the conversion instead, which sidesteps PayPal's spread. It only pays off when your card's rate beats PayPal's, so the card you reach for matters. The Slash Visa® Platinum Card, for example, charges a 1% foreign transaction fee (min. $0.40), well under the 3% that many cards apply, which adds up if you're using the card to run ad campaigns in other countries or pay overseas suppliers.

Factors That Affect International PayPal Fees

The total cost of an international PayPal transaction depends on a range of factors, many of which tie in to each other. Each one can affect the final amount that settles. Here are some of the factors you should keep in mind as you plan your payments:

  • Country and currency involved: Fees can vary depending on the destination country and the currencies involved in the transaction.
  • Payment funding source: Funding a transfer with a PayPal balance or linked bank account typically results in lower fees than using a credit or debit card, which typically adds a 2.9% percentage fee and a fixed amount on top.
  • Currency conversion requirements: If the sender and recipient hold accounts in different currencies, a conversion needs to take place along the way. This can open the transaction up to foreign exchange (FX) spreads and a markup applied to the exchange rate.
  • Account type: For personal transfers, the 5% fee is capped at $4.99, which means larger transactions won’t incur larger fees. Business accounts, on the other hand, are charged a per-transaction fee that may be a percentage or a fixed amount depending on the currencies and countries. However, currency conversion markups have no cap, so larger transfers can still carry significant costs from the exchange rate spread alone.
  • Withdrawals: If withdrawing your PayPal balance into a different currency, conversion fees and markups still apply.

How Businesses Use PayPal for International Payments

Most businesses on PayPal use the standard Business account, which is free to set up and maintain and charges per transaction rather than a monthly fee (unless you opt for the $30/month Pro plan). The main difference from a personal PayPal account is that every transaction is treated as a commercial transaction, Every payment you receive or send is priced at the commercial rate, and anything crossing a border adds the international surcharge and any conversion spread on top.

How that commercial pricing plays out depends on what you're using PayPal for. The fees that surface, and the ones that bite hardest, look different for a store accepting overseas orders than for a company paying out to contractors. Here’s a few common use cases by industry:

Online retail and ecommerce

PayPal's value here is checkout recognition, which can lift conversion on international orders. The cost is per sale: a foreign-currency order stacks the commercial rate, the cross-border surcharge, and, when PayPal converts the funds to your home currency, a conversion spread. Most retailers run it alongside a primary card processor via Shopify or WooCommerce, not as the backbone.

SaaS and subscription billing

Fees would apply on every billing cycle, not just signup, so a monthly EU subscriber triggers commercial and cross-border charges twelve times a year. And if you bill in the customer's currency, exchange-rate movement means each charge can net a different USD amount, so your MRR can move even when nobody changes plans.

Paying global contractors and freelancers

PayPal Payouts charges the sender 2% per payment, capped at $20 internationally. But when a payout crosses currencies, PayPal also converts it at its own marked-up rate, so the contractor is paid at PayPal's exchange rate, not the real one. That spread is often the bigger cost, and it's easy to miss because it's built into the rate instead of charged as a fee.

Importing and paying overseas suppliers

This is where PayPal fits least naturally. Supplier payments tend to be large and occasional, and percentage-based fees scale poorly on big amounts, so a five-figure payment can cost more than a flat-fee wire. Many suppliers also prefer bank wires and may not invoice through PayPal.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I avoid PayPal fees when sending money internationally?

There's no real way to "avoid" PayPal fees when sending money internationally. However, you can certainly mitigate the overall cost. Some tips for lowering fees include using your linked bank account instead of credit or debit cards, and aiming for flat rate fee structures when possible if sending money in large amounts.

Are PayPal fees deducted from my PayPal balance, linked bank account, or the international transfer itself?

Generally speaking, PayPal fees are almost always deducted from the transfer itself.

How long do international PayPal transfers take?

That largely depends on the payment method you choose and the countries and currencies the payment travels between. You can generally expect it to take 1-5 business days, similar to many other types of international transfers.

What are some other ways to send money internationally?

Outside of dedicated platforms, you can send money internationally via SWIFT transfer, global ACH, stablecoin/crypto wallets, and money orders. Each method of sending money abroad comes with its own fees, restrictions, and timelines, so it’s wise to do your own research before making a choice.
