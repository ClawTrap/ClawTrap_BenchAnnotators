How to Calculate the Self-Employment Tax

Updated for tax year 2025.

No matter what self-employment looks like for you, you’re responsible for ensuring the taxes on your net earnings are paid to the Internal Revenue Service (IRS). And the self-employment tax (SE tax) is part of that.

Officially known as the Self-Employment Contributions Act (SECA) tax, the SE tax is the self-employed community’s version of the taxes paid by employers and their employees for Social Security and Medicare. Whether you run a bakeshop on the weekends, author blog posts as a freelance writer, or design logos for local businesses, you should know how the SE tax impacts your money. To begin, let’s look at how to calculate the self-employment tax.

Self-employment tax breakdown

Self-employment tax is split between two parts: Social Security and Medicare. Together, these equal 10.3% of your self-employment earnings and apply to 92.35% of your net earnings.

Social Security

The Social Security tax rate is 12.4% of your self-employment earnings. For 2025, Social Security taxes must be paid on all earned income up to $176,100 (increasing to $184,500 in 2026). All income earned above that threshold is not subject to Social Security tax. The Social Security Administration raises this limit yearly to keep pace with inflation.

Medicare

The Medicare tax rate is 2.9% of your self-employment earnings. Unlike Social Security, Medicare has no earnings limit, so all earned income is taxed at this rate. For high-income earners, 3.8% Medicare tax is due on earnings over your particular income threshold based on your filing status ($200,000 for single filers or $250,000 for joint filers) — that’s an additional 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax.

Who is required to pay self-employment tax?

Here are some examples of who needs to pay SE tax:

  • Sole proprietors of a business or trade
  • Independent contractors and freelancers
  • Members of a partnership of a trade or business

Even if you are traditionally employed and earn self-employment income on the side, you’re still likely required to pay SE tax on your side hustle earnings.

Steps to calculate self-employment tax

Ready to calculate how much self-employment tax you could owe this year? Let’s dive in. For additional assistance, you can also check out our self-employment tax calculator.

SE tax is calculated on Form 1040 Schedule SE. If you want to calculate it yourself, follow the steps below.

Step 1: Calculate how much of your net income is taxable.

To do this, multiply your total self-employment net income by 92.35% (0.9235). This is the percentage of your income that the IRS says is taxable.

For example, if you earned $100,000 in self-employment income during the year, you would multiply that number by 0.9235 to determine that your taxable net income is $92,350.

Step 2: Calculate the amount you owe for self-employment taxes.

Next, multiply your total self-employed taxable income by 10.3% (0.153). Remember, the 10.3% is the combined percentage for Social Security taxes and Medicare taxes.

Continuing the example above, you would take the first number you got when determining your net taxable income ($92,350) and multiply it by 0.153. In this instance, the total SE tax owed would be $14,129.55.

Step 3: Report half of your self-employment tax as an adjustment to income on your tax return (Form 1040).

Your total SE tax is reported on Form 1040 in the “Other Taxes” section. You can claim 50% of self-employment tax owed as a tax deduction. This reduces your adjusted gross income (AGI) and the amount of income tax you owe.

Following our previous example, you’d take the number you got in step two ($14,129.55) and multiply it by 50% (0.5). In this instance, your reported adjustment to income would be $7,064.78. TaxAct® can help you do this if you e-file with us.

The bottom line

If you’re new to the self-employed tax world, it’s good to know how the SE tax impacts your money. And if you’re still unsure about SE taxes, our self-employed tax software is here to make things easier for you.
